Cite Your Project

Part 6 of 6 · Unit 2 Lab 5. Everything you have learned, applied to the thing you will actually be graded on. Below is a made-up student project. Your job is to work out what has to be credited — and what does not.

The project: Skyhop

A one-screen platformer. Your character runs and jumps between platforms collecting coins, and the game ends when you fall off the bottom.

Here is the main script. It looks like plenty of CSP projects — which is the point.

when green flag clicked
switch to costume hopper
go to x: 0 y: -120
repeat until y position < -180
handle gravity and platform collision
if touching coin?
play sound coin-ding
add 1 to score
show score banner
wrap around edges
say Game over

Where each piece came from

Seven ingredients. For each one, decide with your partner before you reveal. Not all of them need a citation — part of the skill is knowing which.

How to actually write it

Different kinds of borrowing get credited differently. For anything under a Creative Commons license, the four things you need are Title, Author, Source, License — TASL.

What you borrowedWhat to write
Image or sound
CC licensed		 "Title" by Author, from Source, licensed CC BY 4.0
Image or sound
public domain / CC0		 "Title" by Author, from Source (CC0 — no credit required, given anyway)
Code from a website Block adapted from [page title], [url], accessed [date]
Code from a person Block written by [name], used with permission
Code from an AI tool Generated with [tool], [date], from the prompt: "[your prompt]"

Where it goes in Snap!: right-click the block → add comment, and put the credit on the block itself. Then collect every credit in one comment on the stage, so a reader can see all of it in one place without hunting.

The part people get wrong

Citing borrowed code does not make it yours.

On the real Create Task you are asked to show code you developed. Anything you borrowed — even perfectly cited — cannot be the thing you point to as your own work. So the gravity block is fine to use and fine to cite, but it cannot be the algorithm you present.

There is a second trap. You are expected to be able to explain what your program does. Pasting in a block you cannot read is risky even when you credit it honestly.

And the reason to be scrupulous is not only honesty: clear citation is what makes the rest of the project visibly yours.

Check the exact wording of this year's requirements with your teacher before you submit — College Board updates the task, and the rule that matters is the one in force when you turn it in.
