Locked

Part 5 of 6 · Unit 2 Lab 5. Fair use is a right you have. This page is about what happens when the thing you have a right to do is behind a lock.

One week in October 1998

27 October 1998

Copyright Term Extension Act

Copyright gets 20 years longer. You met this one on the Copyright Clock page — it is what Eric Eldred sued over, and what Creative Commons was built in response to.

28 October 1998

Digital Millennium Copyright Act

Signed the next day. Among other things it made it illegal to break a digital lock on a copyrighted work — even when what you wanted to do with the work was perfectly legal.

Two laws, one day apart. One made copyright last longer; the other let companies put locks on it and called picking them a crime. Creative Commons answers the first. Nothing much answers the second.

Your stuff

You paid for all of these. Try to do something ordinary with each one.

So pick the lock

DMCA section 1201 makes it illegal to get around a technological measure that controls access to a copyrighted work — and illegal to make or share a tool that does it.

Read that again, because of what it does not say. It does not say “illegal to infringe.” Breaking the lock is its own offence, separate from whether your actual use was legal. Your fair use right survives. Your ability to exercise it does not.

What you are allowed to do

Quote a film in an essay. Make an accessible copy for a blind student. Repair a machine you own. Back up what you bought.

What section 1201 does about it

Nothing — those uses stay legal. But if a lock stands between you and doing them, removing the lock is a separate violation. The lock outranks the right.

There is a release valve, and it is a strange one. Every three years the Librarian of Congress holds hearings and grants temporary exemptions — past ones have covered things like repairing devices, unlocking phones, accessibility, preservation and classroom film clips. They expire. People have to show up and ask for them again. A right you must re-apply for every three years is a different kind of right.

Cory Doctorow's three laws

From Information Doesn’t Want to Be Free (2014). You met Doctorow on the license page, giving a novel away on the day it went on sale. He spent years at the Electronic Frontier Foundation fighting the other half of 1998.

  1. Any time someone puts a lock on something that belongs to you and won’t give you the key, that lock isn’t there for your benefit. Test it against the shelf above. For each lock, ask who it actually protects.
  2. Fame won’t make you rich, but you can’t get paid without it. Why he gave the book away. For a new writer the enemy is obscurity, not copying.
  3. Information doesn’t want to be free. People do. A correction to the old slogan. Information wants nothing; it is not alive. The argument was always about people.

In your pair doc

  • Pick one lock from the shelf. Who does it protect, and who does it cost? Use Doctorow’s first law.
  • A teacher wants to make an audio version of a locked textbook for a blind student. The use is legal. Explain, in your own words, why she still may not be allowed to do it.
  • The two 1998 laws were signed a day apart. Creative Commons was built to answer the first one. Could something like CC answer the second? Why or why not?
  • Doctorow says information doesn’t want to be free, people do. What does he gain by changing the sentence that way?
